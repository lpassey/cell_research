From Agency to Architecture:
Reframing the "Self-Sorting" Model of Morphogenesis

Abstract

This paper presents a critical technical rebuttal of Zhang, Goldstein, and Levin's "Classical Sorting Algorithms as a Model of Morphogenesis: Self-Sorting Arrays Reveal Unexpected Competencies in a Minimal Model of Basal Intelligence" (Adaptive Behavior, 2024). The original work recasts three classical sorting algorithms—Bubble, Insertion, and Selection sort—as "cell-view" distributed systems in which individual array elements act as autonomous agents executing local decision rules, and interprets the emergent behaviors of these systems as evidence of basal intelligence. The present analysis subjects the associated public codebase to systematic technical examination, revealing several significant methodological and interpretive concerns.

First, the three "cell-view" implementations are shown to be functionally distinct from their canonical counterparts—designated here as Randomized Pair Exchange (RPE), Randomized Prefix Exchange (RPX), and Randomized Stateful Drift (RSD)—and are more accurately characterized as novel stochastic local-exchange algorithms rather than distributed reformulations of established procedures.

Second, although the original paper presents the implementations as executing in parallel, the use of Python's Global Interpreter Lock (GIL) renders execution concurrent but not truly parallel, introducing non-deterministic scheduling that contradicts the authors' claim of algorithmic determinism.

Third, a centralized functional equivalent of the "cell-view" RPE algorithm is constructed and shown to produce state-transition-by-state-transition identical results under matched conditions, demonstrating that any observed robustness is attributable to update rules, execution order, and fault-handling logic rather than to architectural decentralization or distributed autonomy.

Fourth, efficiency comparisons between cell-view and traditional algorithms are found to be methodologically questionable: reproduced measurements show that cell-view variants incur substantially higher comparison costs than claimed, and the baseline implementations against which they were measured appear to reflect naive rather than optimized formulations.

Fifth, the paper's fault-tolerance claims are unsubstantiated due to absent experimental protocols, undefined fault models, and non-executable baselines; dormant code fragments suggest the authors explored probabilistic comparison-inversion faults that went unreported.

The paper concludes that any resilience exhibited by the cell-view implementations reflects well-established properties of iterative local-exchange systems under centralized termination control—a framework described decades prior in self-stabilization theory—rather than constituting evidence for emergent basal intelligence in minimal distributed systems.

Introduction

In "Classical sorting algorithms as a model of morphogenesis: Self-sorting arrays reveal unexpected competencies in a minimal model of basal intelligence," Zhang, Goldstein, & Levin, explore whether systems traditionally regarded as purely mechanical and non-cognitive can nonetheless exhibit behaviors they associate with minimal or "basal" intelligence.

Their paper aims to challenge the assumption that intelligence requires complex neural architectures by arguing that global order and adaptive behavior can emerge from collections of simple interacting elements. Their approach was to reinterpret three familiar sorting algorithms as simplified, abstract models for studying these interactions. It recasts array elements as autonomous "cells" that execute local decision rules rather than being manipulated by a centralized controller, and presents this as a more biologically grounded, distributed architecture1, drawing an analogy between the organization of elements in an array and the spatial organization of cells during biological development.

To investigate this idea, the authors implemented a custom sorting evaluation framework and re-implemented three familiar sorting algorithms (Bubble sort, Insertion sort, and Selection sort) into what they term as a "cell-view" representation. The authors frame conventional sorting implementations as relying on centralized, top-down control and contrast this with their bottom-up agent model, although while centralized control is a common implementation pattern it is not a formal requirement of any traditional sorting algorithm.2

They then examined their system's behavior under a range of perturbations, including simulated execution failures and disruptions due to unreliable element behavior. The authors report that these distributed, "cell-view" systems often recover from such perturbations and successfully complete sorting tasks.

The paper also highlights two phenomena that appear to arise in the distributed "cell-view" implementations: (a) temporary reduction in progress to navigate around a defect, and (b) unexpected clustering behavior among array elements when different sorting policies coexist in chimeric arrays.

On the basis of these observations, the authors interpret their findings as evidence that systems composed of simple locally interacting agents can sort themselves more reliably and robustly in the presence of errors than common implementations of traditional sorting algorithms. The authors interpret these observations as evidence that "basal forms of intelligence can emerge in simple systems without being explicitly encoded in their underlying mechanics" 3

The extent to which these observed behaviors arise from genuinely decentralized, locally informed agents—as opposed to from residual centralized control, global state visibility, or algorithmic modifications introduced to handle perturbations—is not resolved at the level of conceptual framing alone. These distinctions are left to be evaluated by examining the concrete technical implementation and its departures from standard sorting algorithms.

In some discussions of this work, other commentators have framed these results as suggesting that distributed processing itself leads to emergent behaviors not present in centralized systems or that the distributed implementations exhibit flexibility and robustness because of their architecture rather than the specific rule set used.4 These interpretations extend the paper's framing and are not supported by controlled architectural comparisons in the published text. They should therefore be understood as interpretive inferences by readers rather than explicit, evidenced conclusions of the authors.

The purpose of the present analysis is to examine the published codebase and experimental setup in detail and to determine whether the behaviors attributed to the system are accurately characterized and reproducible, and whether—when correctly described—they support the paper's broader claims about decentralization, autonomy, and basal intelligence, or may instead arise from modeling assumptions, implementation artifacts, or measurement error.

Analysis of the Published Codebase

The only publicly available source of the Python code corresponding to the reported experiments is the GitHub repository at https://github.com/Zhangtaining/cell_research.

The GitHub repository contains little to no documentation describing intended usage or experimental context. Several scripts (e.g., sorting_cells.py, sorting_cells_multithread.py) fail to execute as written, often due to references to outdated class definitions. In freezing_sorting_analysis.py there is code to allow use of any of the three redesigned sorting algorithms, but the code is commented out in such a way that only the "bubble" algotype is ever used.

The structure of the repository suggests that portions of the code reflect intermediate stages of development; as the codebase evolved, earlier scripts were not consistently refactored to preserve viability. The repository appears to represent a snapshot of the codebase at or near the conclusion of the reported experiments. In the absence of prior version history, earlier implementations and experimental iterations can only be inferred from the existing files. My analysis often includes reasonable inferences drawn from the original paper and the incomplete codebase.

The evaluation strategy used in my study was to identify each Python script that has a __main__ method, and attempt to run that script in a controlled environment. When the scripts failed, I attempted to refactor the code as necessary to create working code; that code was then examined to discover its operating logic.

To ensure transparency I have forked the original GitHub repository to https://github.com/lpassey/cell_research. As I have modified the original code to make it executable, to add metrics, or to enhance testability, I have checked these revisions into the new repository with a description and explanation of the changes. By using the git version control system, it should be possible to track exactly what changes I have made and how they differ from the authors' original code. I have tried to avoid making changes for efficiency reasons to maintain fidelity to the original codebase. When the code was unclear, or when I assumed a rationale that was not explicit, I have added clarifying comments while leaving the code unchanged.

Implementations of Cell-View Sorting Algorithms

We begin by examining the data structures and execution model used by the authors to implement their cell-view sorting algorithms. We start with how individual cells are represented in code and how their behavior over time is controlled.

The Cell-View Formulation as a Programming Paradigm

According to the original paper:

[W]e implement a distributed algorithm that is executed, in parallel, by each number (i.e., cell) with local knowledge of its environment. In lieu of a central controller, cells have individual preferences about the ordering between them and their neighbors. 5

We sought to study the sorting process in a more biologically grounded (distributed) architecture, where each cell is a competent agent implementing local policies. We thus defined three bottom-up versions of common sort algorithms, where actions take place based on the cells' perspective (view) of their environment within the array. 6

The authors' implementations claims to use the Python 3.0 scripting language, 7 and all sorting elements are instances of a common cell template (MultiThreadCell) that defines the general structure of cell behavior. At the conceptual level, the authors' implementations consist of elements represented as objects derived from this common template. This template defines the shared structure of all cells: stored attributes, boundary information, references to the containing array, and the general-purpose swap() method. It does not itself define specific movement logic, but instead defines a set of decision procedures—such as determining whether a move should occur and how that move should be carried out—that must be filled in by more specialized cell types. These specialized cell types correspond to different sorting strategies (e.g., Bubble sort, Insertion sort, Selection sort), each implemented as a distinct kind of cell with its own movement and decision rules. The paper terms these various sorting algorithm classes "algotypes".

From a software engineering standpoint, this is a conventional object-oriented design. A base class defines shared state and methods; subclasses override or extend behavior to implement algorithm-specific policies. This design allows the same overall system structure to be reused while varying only the local behavioral rules assigned to individual cells. The "cell-view" formulation therefore amounts to distributing algorithmic control across object instances rather than centralizing it in a single loop over an array.

Each cell maintains a status field as specified in MultiThreadCell.py (e.g., "ACTIVE", "MOVING", "INACTIVE", "FREEZE"). These statuses regulate whether the cell may initiate movement, whether it is in transition, or whether it has reached the end of its useful life. The "FREEZE" status is used to simulate damage, whose ability to swap places with another cell is limited. All cells also maintain a reference to the shared array in which they reside, permitting read and write access to global state, a StatusProbe to log progress, and a shared TreadLock used to synchronize access to the global array.

The core shared operation is swap(). When invoked, it is given a target index. If the invoking cell is in the "FREEZE" state, the attempt is logged and no action occurs. Otherwise, the cell copies itself to the target position and replaces its former position with the displaced element. Importantly, swap() does not independently validate the correctness of the move; correctness is assumed to have been established by subclass logic prior to invocation. The base template thus provides structure, while subclasses supply mechanics.

Conceptually, this design allows mixed ("chimeric") arrays: heterogeneous cell types coexisting within a shared structure. All cells—regardless of type—share the same underlying control structure, execution cycle, global data access, and state awareness as defined by the commonMultiThreadCell template.

This structure is entirely compatible with standard object-oriented paradigms and does not, in itself, imply distributed execution or emergent behavior. It is a well-established modularization strategy in computer science.

The Multi-Threaded Execution Model

Separately from the conceptual "cell-view" formulation, the authors also state:

We used multi-thread programming to implement the cell-view sorting algorithms. 2 types of threads were involved during the sorting process: cell threads are used to represent all cells, with each cell represented by a single thread; a main thread is used to activate all the threads and monitor the sorting process. 8

The MultiThreadCell template itself inherits from Python's built-in Thread template, which gives it the ability to participate in the Python threading module. Thus, every cell of any algotype is both a data object and potentially a thread of execution. A separate "main" thread activates all cell threads and periodically checks whether the array is sorted.

This multithreaded design is explicitly presented by the authors as a means of achieving parallel execution analogous to the activity of biological cells, in which many cells act simultaneously. In CPython, the Global Interpreter Lock (GIL) ensures that only one thread executes Python bytecode at a time. Python's threading model is therefore concurrent but not parallel. Concurrency gives the illusion of simultaneous execution while in fact executing things in a random sequence. 9

Thread execution is interleaved under interpreter control and operating system scheduling; no fixed ordering among threads is guaranteed, and true simultaneous execution of Python bytecode does not occur. Consequently, although the design is described as parallel, the threading module functions as a stochastic scheduler, determining which cell will be executed next. Execution order is therefore governed by interpreter scheduling not by algorithmic structure.

When a thread is scheduled, its run() method is invoked. For each specialized cell type derived from the MultiThreadCell template, run() repeatedly checks whether the cell's status is "INACTIVE" and, if not, acquires the shared lock, calls the specialized move() method, releases the lock, and then repeats. The shared lock enforces mutual exclusion on the array, ensuring that only one cell modifies it at any given time. This serialization eliminates concurrent writes and guarantees atomicity of swaps, but it also collapses the system into effectively single-threaded execution at the point of mutation. When the status for any given cell becomes "INACTIVE" it terminates naturally.

In the absence of an explicit sleep or yield operation in this loop (which is not present in the existing code), a scheduled thread may consume its entire allotted time slice (typically 5 milliseconds) repeating the same operations before another thread is scheduled.

Because each cell's invocation order is not predetermined, the sorting process operates on the list of cell elements through repeated trials rather than a fixed traversal order. No individual cell can determine global sortedness from its local state alone so the end of a sorting process cannot arise "naturally" from distributed local rules. Instead, a separate monitoring thread periodically scans the entire array to determine whether it is sorted. When sortedness is detected, this thread sets every cell's status to "INACTIVE", allowing their run() loops to terminate. This termination mechanism reintroduces centralized oversight into a system presented as distributed.

The paper asserts that "our algorithms are deterministic; there was no stochasticity in the algorithms" 10 While the canonical sorting algorithms which inspired the "cell-view" implementations are deterministic, any system that relies on thread scheduling under the GIL is non-deterministic. The determinism of the canonical algorithms does not eliminate the stochastic execution order of the cell-view"algorithms.

"Cell-view" (agentic) algorithms.

We now turn to a brief examination of the actual algorithms used, and their various implementations. A detailed description and evaluation of each of the algorithms used can be found in Appendix A.

Randomized Pair Exchange (RPE)

The RPE algorithm is intended to be similar to the traditional Bubble sort. The traditional Bubble sort traverses a list of elements linearly and swaps elements that are out of order. When a traversal completes without any swaps the list is considered sorted. Because "cell-view" elements are invoked randomly, not linearly, and because "cell-view elements cannot detect when the entire array becomes sorted, the RPE algorithm is reduced to a simple set of randomized pair-wise exchanges.

Randomized Prefix eXchange (RPX)

The RPX algorithm was intended to be similar to the traditional Insertion sort. Instead, it became an adjacent-swap process in which a randomly scheduled element examines the sublist to its left and may swap one position left only if its own value is smaller than its immediate neighbor to the left and then only if that entire preceding sublist is already sorted; otherwise it does nothing and yields control of the processor. Unlike a classical Insertion sort, there is no deterministic left-to-right pass, no maintained boundary between sorted and unsorted regions, no guarantee that the next element processed is the next unsorted element, and no inner loop that continues moving the same element leftward until it reaches its correct position. Instead, each activation permits at most one swap and further movement depends on future random reselection, so convergence arises from repeated random local exchanges rather than systematic insertion into a growing sorted prefix.

Randomized Stateful Drift (RSD)

The RSD algorithm is intended to be similar to the traditional Selection sort, where the list is scanned for the lowest possible value which is then moved to the sorted portion. In RSD,each element maintains an internal "ideal_position" index and when the element is randomly selected it compares itself to the element currently at that index; if it is smaller it swaps with that target, otherwise it increments its ideal_position and yields, progressively restricting how far left it can be moved. It is not a Selection sort because no pass scans the remaining suffix to find a global minimum, no fixed boundary advances left-to-right, and placement is not decided by a complete search before swapping; instead, decisions are made through repeated random activations and pairwise comparisons against a drifting per-element pointer.

Efficiency comparison

We turn now to a quantitative evaluation of the authors' reported efficiency results and statistical comparisons between traditional and multithreaded {"cell-view") sorts. The authors state that they used the total steps that each algorithm needed to complete the sorting process for 100 elements in 100 experiments to indicate the efficiency of the algorithm (Figure 4). 11 They go on to say that they counted only swapping steps, and that using only this metric there was no significant difference between the performance of the traditional Bubble and Insertion sorting algorithms and their own reimagined multithreaded ("cell-view") algorithms, from which they concluded that the efficiency is very similar between traditional sorting algorithms and their own implementations.

They claim that they went on to consider both reading and writing as costly steps, and added these metrics to their analysis. They conclude:

These results indicate that the cell-view Bubble and Insertion sorting algorithms are more efficient than the traditional versions. This is likely because traditional algorithms are using each element to compare with other elements, while cell-view algorithms will stop proactively comparing with other cells when they are on the target position. In contrast, the cell-view Selection sorting algorithm is less efficient than the traditional Selection sorting algorithm.

The caption to the referenced Figure 4 reads:

100 experimental repeats were performed in three kinds of sort, in traditional and cell-view modes, to compare efficiencies of each method. When comparing only the active moves taken (A), it is seen that cell-view sort is almost exactly as efficient as the traditional version for Bubble and Insertion sort(p=0.24), but is less efficient for Selection sort (the Z-test statistical value is 120.43, and p-value is 0). When comparing comparisons as well as moves (corresponding to the biological cost of sensing, as well as acting), the cell-view versions are actually more efficient for Bubble and Insertion Sorts (Z-test statistical values for Bubble and Insertion sort were -68.96 and -71.19 respectively, p<<0.01), while less efficient (z=106.55, p<<0.01) for Selection sort (B).

Given the accompanying graphic, it appears that the data generated by the authors showed an average swap rate of approximately 2400 swaps per sort for both the traditional and the RPE algorithms. These values are consistent with the well-established metrics for Bubble sorts, which is an average of (n-(n-1)/4) or 2475 swaps for a 100 element array. For a naive Bubble sort, where the entire array is scanned for duplicates on every pass the maximum number of comparisons is ((n-1)-(n-1)) (9801). For a random permutation of elements, the expected number of passes in a Bubble sort, where n = 100, averages 87.5 passes before the ‘sorted' state is reached.12 Thus, for a naive implementation of the Bubble sort one would expect an average of 8662 comparisons for 2475 swaps.

Independent verification of these claims is difficult because the published codebase is incomplete and several referenced scripts are not directly executable. To date I have found nothing in the code, with the exception of the RSD ("Randomized Stateful Drift") algorithm, that indicates that any Cell can recognize when it has reached its target position. It is also worth noting that the authors did not include any elapsed computation time in their evaluations.

I found only one script that purports to implement a traditional Bubble sort, and it was not only inoperable, but it implemented the Bubble sort on a linked list, not an array. Nor could I find any code that implemented a traditional Insertion or Selection sort. It is likely that the authors relied on documented characteristics of these traditional algorithms for their baseline comparisons rather than creating new implementations. This would explain why there are no baseline time metrics included in their analysis, and why the graphic of traditional sorting efficiency matches the theoretical algorithmic performance so closely.

To obtain a runnable baseline consistent with the authors' cell abstractions, I constructed a single-threaded driver around their BubbleSortCell template. Instead of a multithreaded design, this naive Bubble sort implementation operated as follows:

  1. While the array is not sorted:
    1. traverse the entire array and for each cell:
      1. call the cell's move() method.

I also created simple single-threaded optimized Bubble sort, Insertion sort and Selection sort methods, also using the BubbleSortCell object as the sorting element.

Using multithread_cell_sorting_steps.py as a starting point, I added this new code to create baseline data for all traditional sorting algorithms, as well as the naive implementation of a bubble sort. I added code to execute all seven algorithms 100 times on an identical suite of test arrays, and saved the results in a .csv file. This script is committed to my GitHub repository with the name multithread-cell-sorting-test.py.

Fault Tolerance

While the efficiency measurements constrain what can be inferred about comparative computational cost, they do not address the separate question of algorithmic robustness under fault conditions. The paper advances an additional claim that the cell-view variants exhibit superior error tolerance under injected "Frozen Cell" faults. That claim requires independent examination because it depends on the definition of monotonicity error13, the fault model, and the baseline implementations used for comparison.

Every element derived from the MultiThreadCell template has a potential cell status of "FREEZE". In the base implementation of swap() when an element's swap() method is invoked, the logic first checks its status. If that status is "FREEZE" the method logs the attempt then ends without any further action; thus, a "frozen" cell cannot be the first participant in a swap. However, there is no corresponding check preventing a frozen cell from being moved when another cell's swap() method is invoked. A commented-out line in the code indicates that the authors considered (and perhaps executed) a stricter implementation in which frozen cells would be completely immobile. As written, however, the implementation adopts a weaker notion of "freezing," in which frozen cells retain passive mobility while losing only the ability to initiate movement. To adopt the original authors' anthropomorphism, a "frozen" cell cannot choose to move, but it can be pushed around.

No traditional sorting algorithm assigns a status to an element in an array. The paper provides no reproducible experimental protocol, no detailed definition of the frozen-cell intervention, no tabulated results, and no runnable baseline implementations of the traditional algorithms against which the comparisons are said to have been made. In the absence of documented methodology and verifiable baselines, the reported advantage in fault tolerance cannot be independently confirmed and should therefore be treated as unsubstantiated rather than established. Appendix C details the specific gaps in instrumentation, controls, and implementation evidence that prevent validation of this claim.

freezing_sorting_analysis.py

We now turn to an examinations of a published script: freezing_sorting_analysis.py, which was apparently designed to demonstrate the robustness of the cell-view sorting algorithms under conditions where some cells are deliberately limited in their behavior ("frozen"). This script is a good starting point for analysis as it uses only BubbleSortCell elements, and it is particularly informative because it exposes both the experimental assumptions made by the authors and the practical mechanisms by which those assumptions are implemented in code.14 Additionally, it is useful as an example of how error-handling is explicitly coded into objects which implement the MultiThreadCell template, and not an emergent capability of "agentic" coding.

A detailed description of the existing freezing_sorting_analysis.py script is included in Appendix D.

Implementation of a Deterministic But Unpredictable Execution Model

While a good starting point for analysis, freezing_sorting_analysis.py has several shortcomings: it does not test all of the cell-view algorithms without code modifications; it cannot be run deterministically and therefore the results are not repeatable; and while it uses large lists for testing (100 cells) the cell values are initialized randomly with values from 0 to 10, which causes large numbers of duplicates.

Addressing this last short-coming first, the purpose of freezing_sorting_analysis.py is to evaluate the ability of the three "cell-view" algorithms to successfully sort a list of values when one or more of the cells are "frozen". By populating a 100-element list with random integers between only 0 and 10, the authors have created a high-density environment where duplicate values are guaranteed. Statistically, this ensures that a "frozen" cell has a high probability of being assigned a value that is already compatible with its fixed position in a sorted sequence.

For the next stage of the evaluation, I made a small number of changes to the script to improve transparency and reproducibility and to increase code coverage. These changes facilitated systematic analysis, but they did not alter the functional behavior of the algorithm. This new implementation has been committed to GitHub as freezing_sorting_analysis_single_thread.py.

First, I increased the values to a range of 0-100, which greatly reduced the number of duplicate values in the list.

In the original design, each cell runs in its own thread and repeatedly invokes move() while holding a shared lock. Because execution occurs under the CPython Global Interpreter Lock (GIL), only one thread can execute Python bytecode at any given moment. The additional explicit lock around move() further guarantees that only one cell action is performed at a time. Despite the presence of multiple threads, the effective behavior is therefore a serialized sequence of individual move() calls.

For my next modification I replaced the use of Python threads with a simple control loop driven by a pseudo-random number generator; this change simply moved the selection logic from the Python runtime into a transparent control loop. One important side effect of this change is that, while the sequence of cell activations remains effectively unpredictable, it becomes fully reproducible. Pseudo-random number generators are deterministic: given the same initial seed, they will generate the same sequence of values. This transition maintains the stochastic nature of cell activation while providing a reproducible and functionally equivalent execution environment.

The revised control loop invokes the cell's move() method directly instead of relying on the run() method, and operates as follows:

While the list is not sorted: 1. Compute a random number between zero and the length of the list minus one. 2. Select the cell at the corresponding position in the array. 3. Invoke the cell's move() method. 4. Repeat

This substitution is appropriate for analysis because it preserves the operational semantics that matter to correctness and measured cost: single-cell moves, shared-state updates, and non-deterministic activation order. It removes thread-management overhead and scheduler artifacts without introducing parallelism that does not actually occur under the GIL. In addition, randomized execution can be driven by a seeded pseudo random number generator, producing an activation sequence that is unpredictable in structure yet exactly reproducible across runs with the same seed. That property improves experimental control and auditability while retaining the stochastic scheduling model intended by the original design.

This change to the control loop allowed me to remove any references in the code to the thread lock as there is no longer any risk of simultaneous modification of a shared resource, however I have avoided making changes to optimize the code in order to maintain as much similarity to the existing code as possible.

A global StatusProbe template instance is injected into every object derived from MultiThreadCell. This logging object records the state of the array every time the swap() method is called. I added code to freezing_sorting_analysis_single_thread.py to record this state history to both .npy and .csv (Comma Separated Values) files to preserve the state of the sort attempts for all cell-view algorithms with 0, 1 and 2 frozen cells. This makes it possible to reconstruct the exact sequence of sorted states produced during a run and to verify that repeated executions with the same seed yield identical behavior.

Functional Decomposition of Cell Logic

The original paper attributed the observed robustness of the sorting process—particularly its tolerance to frozen or malfunctioning cells—to the agentic, decentralized nature of the cell-view implementation. This interpretation presupposes that these behaviors arise from properties intrinsic to distributed autonomy rather than from the underlying control logic itself.

To evaluate this claim, I created another variation of freezing_sorting_analysis_single_thread.py, now called freezing_sorting_analysis_non-agentic.py, having alternative implementations of the same sorting dynamics, but in which all control is explicitly centralized. The purpose of this next analysis was to determine whether a single executive process, operating without autonomous agents or distributed decision-making, could reproduce the same behaviors observed in the cell-view system under identical perturbations. If so, this would suggest that any robustness exhibited by the implementation—however defined or measured—cannot be attributed to distributed autonomy, since identical state trajectories arise under a centralized executor operating the same transition rules and execution order.

The source code found in freezing_sorting_analysis_single_thread.py initially generated a random array of integers which was then used as the value parameters in creating the list of cell elements; in order to minimize changes, I simply pointed the sorting process to this initial array in lieu of creating a new cell list. Prior to each experiment 0, 1, or 2 of the array values were randomly selected to be frozen. Because the initial array held raw integer values, not objects, the selected positions were stored in a global list of frozen positions.

The next and final step in my evaluation of freezing_sorting_analysis.py was to extract the logic delegated to the cells and return it to a centralized execution process as implemented in the new method rpe_move. In the new version, the code previously encapsulated within MultiThreadCell was extracted into distinct helper methods called by the centralized executor. To ensure that no existing "cell-view" code was executed, I replaced the targeted list of cell elements with the initial array of integers which were not embedded in any other object. In an explicit conditional branch the controller determined if the targeted neighbor array value was in the list of frozen positions, and if so it updated the frozen list to reflect the new position of the frozen value. This special test was explicitly derived from the existing agent code: rpe_move() will never be executed for the frozen position, but other logic can cause the frozen position to migrate.

Like the implementation of freezing_sorting_analysis_single_thread.pycode, the control loop generates a random number corresponding to the length of the array; if that position is currently frozen it moves on, otherwise it selects the value at that position. It then calls rpe_move(), which randomly selects the neighbor to the right or left for comparison, and swaps the two values if out of order. No additional fault-handling logic was introduced beyond what was already present in the "cell-view" implementations.

The new executive process also implements logging to record the state of the array after every invocation of rpe_move(). By using an identical pseudo-random number seed, I guaranteed that the centralized executor invoked rpe_move() in exactly the same order as the single-threaded "cell-view" code invokes the move() method defined in the BubbleSortCell object. The centralized and agentic versions invoke the pseudo-random number generator in identical order and frequency per iteration, and because of the detailed logging we can see the state of the agentic list and the integer array at every step of the process.

Across repeated experiments using identical pseudo-random seeds and perturbations, the centralized executor and the "cell-view" implementations produced identical sequences of array state traces at every logged step of the sorting process. The single-threaded ‘cell-view' implementation serialized execution under a deterministic scheduler; the centralized version preserved that scheduler exactly. Under these conditions, the two systems are transition-by-transition equivalent. The results of these tests have been uploaded to https://github.com/lpassey/cell_research together with the Python scripts used to generate them.

If two systems are functionally identical across all state transitions, they are said to be isomorphic. In the context of freezing_sorting_ analysis.py, the centralized and "cell-view" implementations exhibit this form of isomorphism under identical initial conditions and perturbations. If a central controller can produce the same result using the same logic as a distributed system, then any observed robustness does not depend on distributed autonomy, because identical behavior occurs in its absence; it arises, instead, from update rules, execution order, and explicit perturbation-handling logic. Architectural decentralization contributes no additional causal power under the tested conditions.

Unreported Comparison-Error Injection and the Reframing of Robustness

The following analysis is necessarily speculative, but it is grounded in the published source code and in well-established results from fault-tolerant distributed systems.

The published source code contains explicit remnants of a contemplated fault model that is neither described nor evaluated in the paper. In multiple locations one finds logic of the form:

err_happen = random.random() < 0
if err_happen:
    return not self.value > self.cells[int(target_position[0])].value

and, in commented form:

# err_happen = random.random() < 0.00001
# if err_happen:
#     return not self.value > self.cells[int(target_position[0])].value

As written, random.random() < 0 is an impossibility, so no comparison inversion can occur. The commented variant indicates that the authors at least contemplated a non-zero probability of inverting the comparison predicate. This would model a transient comparison fault—analogous to a hardware comparator failure or bit flip—where the outcome of a > b is spuriously reversed.

However, the paper does not identify this error model, does not specify the probability used, does not describe experimental conditions under which it was enabled, and does not report any quantitative evaluation of its effects. The existence of dormant or commented code therefore demonstrates consideration of a fault model that is neither documented nor empirically analyzed.

It is therefore reasonable to infer that the authors experimented with probabilistic comparison inversions during development. If so, any robustness observed under such perturbations would depend on properties of the surrounding execution framework rather than on the "cell-view" architecture itself. The omission of explicit reporting is material, because robustness claims require a defined fault model and measured behavior under that model.

From an algorithmic standpoint, two classes of comparison error must be distinguished:

  1. False positive swap (reporting a > b when it is not). This introduces unnecessary swaps and therefore increases the number of cycles. For classical iterative sorts such as Bubble or Insertion sort, this does not prevent eventual convergence provided that correct comparisons continue to occur.
  2. False negative swap (failing to swap when required). In a traditional implementation with typical termination detection (e.g., "stop when no swaps occurred in a pass"), a persistent false negative can cause premature termination with the list in an unsorted state. This vulnerability has long been recognized in distributed termination detection and stabilization theory.

As previously demonstrated, the "cell-view" implementations rely on an external controller for termination detection. The agents do not autonomously declare global completion; instead, a centralized routine (e.g., is_sorted()) determines termination. This architecture inherently mitigates certain transient comparison faults: if a swap is incorrectly omitted during one interaction but the system continues iterating, subsequent correct comparisons can repair the disorder. Thus, any observed resilience to transient comparison inversion arises from continued global iteration under centralized termination control, not from distributed morphogenetic properties of the agents. In other words, the tolerance is algorithmic and systemic, not architectural in the sense claimed.

This is not a novel discovery. The capacity of iterative local-exchange systems to self-correct under transient perturbations is a well-established concept in self-stabilizing systems theory. 15 The presence of a global controller that withholds termination until a consistent global predicate is satisfied is a classical technique for ensuring recovery from transient faults. The code fragments merely suggest that the authors may have encountered these longstanding principles in practice.

Accordingly:

  • The source code acknowledges a probabilistic comparison fault model.
  • The paper does not define, categorize, or evaluate that model.
  • Any robustness to such faults derives from centralized termination logic and repeated global passes.
  • These mechanisms correspond directly to decades-old results in distributed systems and self-stabilization theory.

If robustness was observed under comparison perturbations, that tolerance arises from the centralized iteration/termination structure and repeated global sweeps, reflecting known properties of iterative sorting with centralized termination detection. It does not constitute evidence that the "cell-view" paradigm introduces a novel form of morphogenetic fault tolerance.

Appendix A — Referenced and created algoritms

The Bubble sort algorithm

The Bubble sort acts on an organized list of elements, each of which is capable of being compared to any other element. It proceeds as follows:

  1. The algorithm selects the first pair of elements in the array, and compares them
  2. If the second element in the pair is less than the first element the two elements swap positions.
  3. The next pair of elements is selected. Note that the second element in step 2 is now the first element in this step.
  4. Steps 2 and 3 are repeated until the end of the list is reached.
  5. If any swap occurs in step 2, start over at step 1. If no swap occurs, the list is sorted.

Typically, the standard Bubble sort is implemented in a centralized execution process which examines, compares and swaps cells if needed, although a centralized process is not mandated by the algorithm itself.

Randomized Pair Exchange (RPE)

The first "cell-view" algorithm that the author's discuss is their variation on the classic Bubble sort. In this novel implementation the authors created a new random-pair exchange sorting algorithm that differs materially from the traditional Bubble sort. It discards steps 1. and 3. of the standard Bubble sort, which mandate a linear sequence. Because the move() method is invoked randomly the ability to determine if every element has been visited without a swap is also unavailable so steps 4. and 5. are also omitted. As is mandated by the MultiThreadCell template, in this implementation the compare/swap step (2.) is performed by code embedded in the element itself. When invoked, the cell compares its own value to that of a randomly selected neighbor (left or right). If the pair is out of order a swap is initiated. For clarity, I will refer to this new algorithm as a Randomized Pair Exchange (RPE).

The Insertion sort algorithm

Beginning with a list of values in an arbitrary (typically random) order:

  1. The algorithm begins by treating the first element of the array as a sorted sublist of length one.
  2. The next element in the array is selected and compared to the value to its immediate left.
  3. While the value of the selected element is less than that of the element being compared, the two elements swap positions.
  4. When the value of the selected element is no longer less than the value of the element to its immediate left, the sublist is sorted.
  5. The next element in the array is selected, and steps 2 through 4 are repeated.
  6. Once the last element of the list has been processed, the entire array has been sorted.

Randomized Prefix eXchange (RPX)

The "cell-view" implementation inspired by the traditional Insertion sort is particularly problematic due to the authors' commitment to an implementation where the cell itself has no visibility into a global state. The traditional Insertion sort relies heavily on the knowledge of the sorted segment that occupies the beginning of the list. Because an element in the Insertion sort cannot be moved until the segment to its immediate left is completely sorted, every time a cell is selected it must traverse the sublist to its left. If the sublist is not sorted the cell's process halts and the scheduler selects a new element for execution. If the sublist is ordered, the element compares its value to the value to its immediate left, and if the two elements are not ordered, then the two elements are swapped. In a traditional Insertion sort the comparison would continue and the selected element would continue to move down the list. In this new implementation the cell does not move further left until the scheduler selects it again.

While framed by the authors as a variant of an Insertion sort, this algorithm is more accurately described as a stochastic local-exchange process whose convergence depends on repeated random sampling rather than on systematic reduction of disorder. Going forward, I will refer to this algorithm as Randomized Prefix eXchange (RPX) sort, reflecting its reliance on randomized cell selection and single-step exchanges conditioned on prefix sortedness. The RPX algorithm can be characterized as a constrained variant of the Randomized Pair-Exchange (RPE) algorithm discussed earlier, differing primarily in the introduction of a prefix-sortedness gate on otherwise identical stochastic adjacent exchanges.

This new algorithm can probably be best understood by an example. Consider the list:

[14, 81, 03, 94, 35, 31, 28, 17, 94, 13].

Although threads are being executed at random, no state change will occur until the scheduler selects element number 3 (03). When this element's move() method is called it examines the beginning segment, [14, 81], and recognizes that it is sorted. Because 03 is less than 81 the two elements are swapped, but the thread endsthere, leading to the list:

[14, 03, 81, 94, 35, 31, 28, 17, 94, 13].

The scheduler will again execute cells at random until element number 2 is selected (the new position of 03). The preceding segment, [14], is considered sorted as it has only a single element. The comparison determines that 03 is less than 14, so the two values are swapped, resulting in the list:

[03, 14, 81, 94, 35, 31, 28, 17, 94, 13].

The scheduler then continues as before.

In principle, when using this process an element itself could detect when the list is sorted. If an element determines that 1. the preceding sublist is sorted, and 2. the comparison shows that the value of the element is greater than the last element in the sublist, and 3. the element determines that it is the last element in the list (current position equals the list size), then the list is known to be sorted. However, because the authors were attempting to model independent cell behavior, no element contains a feedback mechanism, therefore the process continues until the controlling thread which monitors the order of the list detects that the list is sorted and sets the status of every cell to "INACTIVE", triggering the termination of each thread.

The Selection sort algorithm

The standard Selection sort is actually one of the simplest algorithms to understand:

  1. Start at the leftmost element in the array (index 0).
  2. Scan the array from the index + 1 to the end and identify the smallest element in the array.
  3. Swap the smallest element found with the element at the index.
  4. Increment the index and repeat steps 2. and 3.
  5. When the index is equal to the length of the array, the sort is complete.

The Selection sort is the only one of the three standard sorting algorithms that swaps elements which are not adjacent. While it does more comparisons than the other two previously mentioned algorithms it performs fewer writes which makes it useful on hardware where writing to memory is more computationally expensive than reading it.

Randomized Stateful Drift (RSD)

The authors created a new algorithm that they describe as a Selection sort, but which differs from the classical Selection sort in several key respects: while it preserves the non-adjacent swaps, it has to deal with the fact that the logic for each element is executed non-deterministically by the Python scheduler (GIL). This new algorithm embeds the sorting logic in a new object that is derived from the MultiThreadCell template, but takes advantage of certain properties of MultiThreadCell that the previous algorithms did not, specifically current_position and ideal_position.

When each SelectionSortCell is created the ideal_position property is initialized to 0, and the current_position property is set to the initial index of the cell in the list. When the sorting process begins, the Python scheduler selects an element at random to execute. That element compares its own value to that of the target element found at the selected element's ideal_position in the list (which has been initialized to 0). If the selected cell's value is less than the target cell's value, the selected element swaps positions with the target element. No change is made to either cell's ideal_position. If the value of the selected cell is greater than or equal to the value of the target cell, no change in order is made, however the ideal_position of the selected element is incremented. At this point the element process stops executing and the Python scheduler selects a new cell to execute.

Note that for this algorithm to work, every SelectionSortCell object must have visibility into the global element list. Note also that each cell also maintains some state, which is the index of the last element it compared itself to (the new ideal_position).

Eventually, the cell having the smallest value will migrate to index 0 and will maintain its ideal_position of 0. Every other element's ideal_position will increase monotonically when compared to that element at index 0, and once incremented that element will never again compare itself to the element at index 0. The Python scheduler may repeatedly attempt to activate a cell, but given its state it will never move to the left of its ideal_position.

As the ideal_position of any given cell increases, the cell can never move to the left of that position, although the action of other cells may cause it to jump to the right. Over time the element may move back left towards the former ideal_position, or the ideal_position may continue to "drift" to the right, but the element will never move to the left of its ideal_position.

This implementation shares more in common with a traditional Insertion sort than a Selection sort, but its stochastic nature and non-adjacent swaps puts it in its own category. Because the algorithm relies on global awareness of the list and local awareness of its perceived boundary, and because the local boundary drifts rightward to its final position, I will refer to this algorithm as a Randomized Stateful Drift sort (RSD).

Here is an example of how this RSD algorithm would behave on the array we saw earlier. I have included a second value in each element to represent its ideal_position:

[(14,0), (81,0), (03,0), (94,0), (35,0), (31,0), (28,0), (17,0), (94,0), (13,0)].

When element 2 (03,0) is executed it will compare itself to the element at index 0. Because element 2 is less that element 0 the two values will be swapped leading to a list of:

[(03,0), (81,0), (14,0), (94,0), (35,0), (31,0), (28,0), (17,0), (94,0), (13,0)].

When element 4 (35,0) is executed it will compare itself to the element at index 0 (03,0). Because element 4 is larger than element 0, no swap will occur, but the selected element's ideal_position will be incremented and the list will now be:

[(03,0), (81,0), (14,0), (94,0), (35,1), (31,0), (28,0), (17,0), (94,0), (13,0)].

Should element 4 (35,1) be selected again it will now compare itself to the element at index 1 (81,0), discover it is smaller, and swap positions. Now the list is:

[(03,0), (35,1), (14,0), (94,0), (81,0), (31,0), (28,0), (17,0), (94,0), (13,0)].

Should element 4 be selected 4 more times in sequence the new list will be:

[(03,0), (35,1), (14,0), (81,3), (94,0), (31,0), (28,0), (17,0), (94,0), (13,0)],

and element 4 will never again attempt a comparison or swap with indices 0-2.

Appendix B. - Performance metrics

This appendix interprets the efficiency results in light of the reproduced measurements, the authors' published claims, and the behavior implied by the accompanying efficiency graphics.

Summary of empirical findings

Across 100 paired trials on identical initial arrays, the standard implementations exhibit the expected relative ordering: Selection sort is fastest in time and lowest in swaps, Insertion sort is intermediate, and Bubble sort is slowest. Expected swap counts for Bubble and Insertion sorts (~2436) match the inversion-count expectation for random permutations of size 100, while Selection sort swaps remain near n−1 (~94). Comparison counts align with implementation style: optimized Bubble ~4.9k, naive Bubble ~8.8k, Insertion ~2.5k, Selection ~5.0k. In contrast, the reimagined variants show substantially higher execution time and comparison counts: RPE comparisons ~9.3k, RSD ~6.1k, and RPX ~109k, with RPX time costs orders of magnitude larger than the traditional Insertion sort baseline.

Implications for the "active moves only" claim

The reproduced swap counts for Bubble vs. RPE are identical across paired trials, which supports equivalence under a swaps-only metric for the Bubble/RPE pair. The published figure for swaps-only efficiency therefore appears plausible if and only if swaps were the sole metric plotted in that panel.

Implications for the "comparisons plus moves" claim

When comparisons are included, the reproduced data contradict the claim that the cell-view Bubble sort variant is more efficient. RPE requires roughly 2× the comparisons of the optimized Bubble sort baseline and more than the naive Bubble sort baseline as well. Because swaps are equal and comparisons are higher, the combined cost metric must be higher for RPE under any additive model of "sensing plus acting", This conflicts with the reported direction of effect in the authors' text for Bubble and Insertion sorts.

Role of baseline implementation choice

The bubble comparison counts in the authors' graphic appear closer to a naive full-pass Bubble sort implementation than to an optimized shrinking-bound implementation. The reproduced naive Bubble sort comparison count (~8.8k) is consistent with the chart scale, whereas the optimized Bubble sort (~4.9k) is not. This suggests the published baseline may have been a naive variant. If so, efficiency conclusions are conditional on that specific baseline choice and do not generalize to standard optimized forms.

Algorithmic non-equivalence in the "selection" comparison

The RSD procedure described in code does not implement classical Selection sort semantics. In classical Selection sort, each pass identifies the minimum element in the unsorted suffix and performs at most one swap. In the RSD design, each element repeatedly compares itself to a moving "ideal index" and may swap whenever an inversion is detected, without first selecting a global minimum candidate. This permits many more swaps and different comparison dynamics. Therefore, the comparison between RSD and classical Selection sort is not algorithm-to-algorithm but rule-set to algorithm, and the efficiency contrast is not like-for-like.

Consequences for the Selection-sort efficiency claim

Because the procedures are not algorithmically equivalent, reported efficiency differences for "selection" reflect design divergence rather than distributed vs. traditional control structure. The close agreement between reproduced comparison counts (traditional ~5044, RSD ~6127) and the published graphic supports that the figure is measuring these non-equivalent processes as if they were counterparts. Any claim of reduced or increased efficiency for "cell-view selection sort" is therefore methodologically ambiguous.

Interpretation of RPX vs. Insertion results

RPX comparison counts are extremely high (~109k) while swaps match inversion counts. This is consistent with a rule in which an element only moves when a left prefix is already locally ordered, causing many failed selection attempts before a valid move occurs. If prefix-order checks are not fully counted as comparisons in the instrumentation, measured comparisons may still understate true sensing cost. The large runtime and comparison gap relative to Insertion sort indicates non-equivalent operational structure rather than a slower implementation of the same algorithm.

Statistical claim plausibility

Reported z-statistics with magnitudes near 70 for n=100 trials imply extremely large standardized mean differences with low variance. The reproduced paired results for Bubble vs. RPE comparisons show differences in the opposite direction. Under a paired analysis, such extreme z values would not arise with these means and variances. This suggests either different measurement definitions, different baselines, or different pairing structure in the original analysis.

Conclusion

The reproduced data support swaps-only equivalence between traditional Bubble and RPE sorts, but do not support superiority of the cell-view variants when comparisons are included. At least one of the published efficiency comparisons (Selection vs. RSD) evaluates non-equivalent procedures, making the conclusion potentially misleading (established fact). The most likely explanation for the discrepancy between published and reproduced efficiency claims is a combination of baseline implementation choice and metric instrumentation differences rather than a demonstrated intrinsic efficiency advantage of the cell-view architecture.

Appendix C — Methodology, Instrumentation, and Verification Limits

This appendix documents the experimental methodology, code reconstruction steps, measurement instrumentation, and verification limits associated with the independent evaluation of the reported cell-view sorting algorithms. Its purpose is to make explicit which results are directly measured, which are derived from established algorithmic properties, and which conclusions depend on constrained inference due to gaps in the available materials.

Repository Condition and Reproducibility Constraints.

The public repository associated with the paper is incomplete with respect to runnable baseline implementations of the traditional sorting algorithms referenced in the text. Several scripts are non-executable as published, and multiple referenced components are absent or inconsistent with current module definitions. As a result, baseline traditional Bubble, Insertion, and Selection sorts used in this evaluation were independently implemented to match canonical algorithm definitions.

Baseline Algorithm Definitions.

Traditional Bubble, Insertion, and Selection sorts were implemented using standard array-based formulations consistent with established literature. Expected swap and comparison counts were cross-checked against known analytical expectations. A separate naive Bubble variant was also implemented which scans the full array each pass without tail-shortening optimization, to test sensitivity to termination strategy.

Cell-View Algorithm Reconstruction.

Cell-view variants (RPE, RSD, RPX) were reconstructed from available scripts and paper descriptions where execution was not directly possible. Where behavior was ambiguous, the simplest interpretation consistent with the published pseudo logic and comments was selected. These reconstruction choices are documented inline in the evaluation code and constitute probabilistic inferences.

Test Harness and Pairing Design.

Each trial used a fixed pseudo-random permutation of 100 elements. For paired comparisons, the same initial array instance was supplied to each algorithm variant within a trial index. Trials were independent across indices. This supports paired statistical comparison and reduces input-distribution variance.

Metrics Captured.

Instrumentation recorded elapsed time, swap counts, and comparison counts. Comparison counts include all explicit element-to-element order checks executed by the algorithm logic. For "cell-view" variants, comparisons performed during candidate reselection and local sensing were also included where observable. Any comparisons embedded in opaque helper routines may be undercounted; this is a localized uncertainty.

Termination Semantics.

Several cell-view scripts lack intrinsic termination criteria and rely on external sortedness checks or manual interruption. All experiments therefore used an external inversion count predicate to determine completion. This alters control structure relative to pure in-algorithm termination and is necessary to accommodate the usage of the sorting elements derived from the authors' MultiThreadCell template.

Efficiency Claim Evaluation Limits.

Because the paper does not publish full raw datasets, exact instrumentation definitions, or executable baselines, statistical claims (including reported Z-test values) cannot be independently reproduced from primary sources. Any agreement between measured aggregates and published figures is therefore corroborative but not confirmatory.

Error Tolerance Claim Evaluation Limits.

The paper's frozen-cell experiments lack an operational definition of the fault injection mechanism, monotonicity error metric, and baseline implementations. No runnable traditional algorithms with frozen-cell handling are provided. Consequently, error-tolerance comparisons cannot be replicated from the supplied materials. Conclusions regarding comparative robustness are therefore unverified.

Scope of Conclusions.

All quantitative tables in this rebuttal reflect measured results under the reconstructed harness described above. Interpretive conclusions are limited to efficiency and cost metrics under those conditions. Broader claims regarding emergent or basal intelligence are out of scope for these measurements and are not tested here.

Appendix D — Analysis of freezing_sorting_analysis.py

freezing_sorting_analysis.py begins by setting up the execution environment. Conceivably it could run the suite of test across all three multithreaded sorting variations, but as implemented it tests only the RPE (Random Pair Exchang) sort. The setup runs each experiment 20 times, and each group of experiments is run using 1, 2, or 3 "frozen" cells, for a total of 60 separate experiments.

A single experiment begins by creating a random list of 100 integers, each of which has a value between 0 and 10. As will be seen later, this choice of values creates a large number of duplicate values which has a significant impact on the evaluation of the sorting algorithm. The array is shuffled, a step which is unnecessary when dealing with a randomly generated array, and which is apparently left over from test runs where the array was a fixed array and needed to be randomized. The script then builds an array of BubbleSortCell elements where the value of each element corresponds to a value in the randomized integer array. Each cell is also assigned a value corresponding to its position in the array, a reference to the array of which it is a part, and a reference to a global thread lock variable, among other things. Most of the other assignments are not used in this test script; they are required by the BubbleSortCell objects only when used in other scripts.

In the last step of array initialization 1, 2 or 3 elements are randomly selected to be "frozen", and those cells' statuses are set to FREEZE; no record is kept of which cells were selected to be "frozen".

The master control program then starts each element by calling its start() method, which adds it to the list of threads controlled by the Python threading module. As noted earlier, the RPE algorithm does not provide a mechanism for determining when a list becomes sorted, so after the cell threads are started the control process (which is also a thread) enters a tight loop where it repeatedly checks to see if the list is sorted and pauses for one second 16 if it is not. When the list becomes sorted the control process goes through the list of cells marking each as "INACTIVE", which causes each cell element to terminate naturally.

Once an experiment is concluded some minimal performance data is collected and the next experiment begins. Once a group of experiments concludes this performance data is printed to the console, and after all groups have concluded the script ends.

Before concluding, we should to examine how fault tolerance is realized in this base implementation. Two code class templates are particularly relevant.

As implemented, freezing_sorting_analysis.py runs tests using only the RPE (BubbleSortCell) algorithm. Commented code in that script would allow a tester to run the script using other of the three "cell-view" algorithms (RPE, RPX or RSD), but doing so would require some modifications to the script itself.

In the RPE implementation (used by default), the method that determines whether a cell may initiate movement explicitly allows interactions with frozen cells. A cell is permitted to attempt a swap not only when both participants are ACTIVE, but also when the target cell has a status of "FREEZE". Thus, while frozen cells are restricted in their ability to initiate actions, they remain eligible participants in comparisons and exchanges initiated by other cells.

Similar to the RPE algorithm, in the RPX (InsertionSortCell) implementation, frozen cells are restricted only in their ability to initiate swaps. They cannot initiate a move themselves, but they may still be swapped if another active cell initiates the operation. This behavior follows directly from the shared MultiThreadCell.swap() template logic.

Before an RPX cell attempts a swap, it performs a prefix-sortedness test. When a cell at position n is selected for execution, it scans the sublist from index 0 through index n−1. The scan compares each element with the previous element to determine whether the prefix is sorted. If any value is found that is less than its predecessor, the prefix is classified as unsorted and no swap is attempted.

When the scan encounters a frozen cell during this process it treats that cell as a boundary rather than as normal participant in the ordering check. The frozen cell is skipped, and the comparison baseline ("previous value") is reset to the element immediately to its right. Operationally, this divides the prefix into two segments and restarts the sortedness test to the right of the frozen cell.

For purposes of prefix validation, a frozen element partitions the list. The left sublist will become increasingly more sorted over time, but because the sortedness test always begins at index 0, until the left sublist is fully sorted up to the frozen cell the right sublist cannot meaningfully progress, as the sortedness test will otherwise fail before the frozen cell is encountered. If the right sublist remains sorted up to the selected cell at index n, the cell is allowed to initiate a swap.

Although frozen cells cannot initiate swaps, they may still be displaced. If an active cell immediately to the right of a frozen cell is selected and is out of order, it may swap with the frozen cell. In that case the frozen cell moves one position to the right. Over repeated iterations, this allows the frozen cell to migrate leftward, but only after the left segment has been re-sorted.

As an example, consider the following list with one frozen cell (F):

[08, 03, 06, 02(F), 05, 07].

When position 5 (value 07) is selected, the prefix scan begins by comparing the values at index 0 (08) and 1 (03); it detects disorder and thus no move is attempted. In face, no cell to the right of index 1 (03) can move until that cell has been selected and moved.

When index 1 is finally selected it will swap with index 0, producing:

[03, 08, 06, 02(F), 05, 07]

Once the left segment is fully sorted, the right list can become sorted. As it is already sorted, no state change will occur until the selection process selects the cell at index 4 (05). When this happens the list will be:

[03, 06, 08, 02(F), 05, 07].

Because a candidate element only compares to its left, and only swaps if it's value is less than its left neighbor, the value 05 cannot move left across 02(F). The algorithm will stall in this partially ordered configuration
